Paradigm Shift

Foundation models offer a complementary path to robot generalization alongside advances in action-policy training. Their broad knowledge and reasoning can help robots interpret unfamiliar goals, organize available capabilities, and adapt through feedback. Agentic robotics connects these decisions to physical action, outcome verification, and adaptation around user intent.

From Policy-Centric Robotics to Agentic Robotics

Policy-centric robotics organizes execution around an action policy. Given a task and observations, the policy produces action targets that the robot’s controller carries out, then uses feedback to adjust its next actions. The policy may also reason or predict subtasks; its central role is to turn task inputs into physical behavior.

Agentic robotics organizes work around an agent. It interprets what the user wants, chooses and coordinates skills and tools, checks whether their effects satisfy the goal, and revises its decisions when they do not. This connects deciding what to do with checking what actually happened.

Three Typical Paths to Execution

A decision can reach the body in several ways. Here, S2 denotes the role that decides and verifies, S1 supplies skills and callable modules, and S0 provides the body interface and local control. Their connections give three common execution paths, which can coexist within one task.

Layered Orchestration. S2 invokes skills and callable modules in S1, including action, perception, and motion-planning modules. Action skills supply control targets for S0 to execute.

Direct Control. S2 sends permitted control targets directly to S0 through an action interface. S0 still runs its local real-time control loop.

Code as Policy. S2 writes a program that takes the role of S1 at run time. The program can execute several actions before S2 checks the outcome and decides what to do next.

Reference Framework

These execution paths share a need to connect intent, action, and evidence. Our reference framework makes the responsibilities and interfaces explicit: S2 decides and verifies, S1 supplies skills and callable modules, and S0 maintains local body control. The decomposition is functional: one model may serve several roles, and one role may span several components.

Embodied System 2

The framework shows how decisions reach the body and how evidence returns. We now examine the part responsible for using that evidence: Embodied S2, realized by a foundation model and an embodied harness.

Foundation Model and Embodied Harness

The foundation model supplies knowledge, understanding, and reasoning. The embodied harness makes these capabilities usable in a robot: it assembles the state on which decisions depend and connects model outputs to execution that can be observed and checked. Stronger reasoning still needs current observations and well-defined interfaces.

Before a decision, the context manager and memory combine current observations with task progress and relevant history. The planner maintains a plan that can change after each result; the tool interface exposes skills with explicit inputs, limits, and execution states; the verifier checks their physical effects against the goal. The cup task is complete only when evidence shows that the cup is at the intended place.

The eight abilities below connect this implementation to evaluation. Knowledge, understanding, and reasoning reside mainly in the model; tool use, memory, cross-embodiment, safety, and efficiency depend on the model and harness together.

Whether it succeeds also depends on when it acts and what has changed in the meantime. Physical constraints therefore shape the decision process, and evaluation must test both the model’s abilities and the resulting behavior.

Physical Constraints

Many modern foundation models improve capability by using more compute, often at the cost of longer deliberation. We call this trend monotonic scaling. In the physical world, reaction deadlines and other constraints demand a different objective: greater capability within fixed limits, or capped scaling. This calls for both better models and a redesign of the surrounding system, including how sensing, reasoning, tools, and control work together. In Figure 6, useful progress increases capability while keeping reaction time below the physical deadline.

The layers therefore work at different timescales. S0 maintains fast body control; S1 control skills update actions; S2 decides when the task needs a new plan or judgment. Perception and planning tools run on demand; the reported model latencies come from different tasks.

Many current robotics benchmarks pause simulated time during model inference. This can mask the physical cost of delayed decisions; evaluation for real-world deployment should also keep the world running during inference. Evaluation

These constraints make evaluation a question of both capability and execution. Model-level tests isolate abilities under specified inputs; system-level tests measure what the model, harness, skills, and body achieve together.     From Digital Agents to Physical Agents

    The evaluation gaps also expose assumptions that change when an agent moves from software into the physical world. The observe–act–verify loop still applies, but the conditions that make it reliable must be rebuilt.

    Conditions for Agentic Work

    Coding agents benefit from familiar interfaces, verifiable outcomes, abundant data, and inexpensive environments. A robot instead has to infer state from sensors, check physical effects, learn from costly trials, and recover from actions that may be difficult to undo.

    What Transfers, What Must Be Rebuilt

    The organizing principles still transfer: act on feedback, define tool contracts, maintain durable state, separate execution from judgment, and reuse validated skills. For robots, those principles require interfaces with explicit units and limits, verifiers checked against physical outcomes, embodied data, and simulation validated against reality.

    Road Ahead

    Rebuilding these foundations should expand the work people can entrust to a robot. Progress therefore needs to track the responsibility a robot can reliably carry, as well as the environments in which it can work. This leads from individual primitives and tasks toward coordinated workflows and sustained roles.

    From a Task to a Standing Role

    Consider breakfast. Picking up a cup is one skill; making and serving coffee is a complete task. Preparing breakfast for four adds timing and coordination across tasks. Taking care of breakfast every day adds a continuing responsibility: remembering preferences, keeping supplies ready, and adapting as needs change. The scene below illustrates this ambition; it is a vision of the work we want to delegate.

    Ladder of Delegable Work

    The ladder makes this progression explicit: a primitive, a task, a workflow, and a standing role. Each step adds responsibility for how work is organized and sustained.

    L1 · Primitive: “Pick up the cup.” Execute one local skill and verify its result. Responsibility stays with that single operation.

    L2 · Task: “Make and serve coffee.” Coordinate skills, monitor progress, and recover from failures until the complete task meets its acceptance criteria.

    L3 · Workflow: “Prepare breakfast for four.” Coordinate interdependent tasks, timing, priorities, and shared resources. Responsibility covers the whole workflow, not each task in isolation.

    L4 · Role: “Take care of breakfast each day.” Sustain an agreed responsibility across tasks and workflows until it expires or is withdrawn. Persistent state maintains continuity between completed workflows.

    In unstructured settings, evidence of unaided work is strongest at the task level. Some company demonstrations extend toward workflows, but their reports do not yet establish all the conditions for reliable workflow-level autonomy. Sustained roles remain a target for development and evaluation.

    Expanding the Operating Scope

    A workcell calls for grounding and precise actions within reach and view. A room adds base positioning and changing reachability; a building adds maps, memory, and search; a site adds long routes, coordination with other people and robots, and handovers between areas. Spatial scope complements the responsibility a robot carries.

    Future Research Directions

    Reason Within Physical Deadlines

    Decide when to think, observe again, act, or reuse a skill within the response budget.

    Keep Control Persistent and Verifiable

    Maintain trustworthy state across tasks and spaces, and coordinate work with shared resources.

    Adapt Across Environments and Bodies

    Reuse verified experience, expose capabilities explicitly, and reduce data and configuration needs.

    Improve from Physical Experience

    Retain changes that help later work without eroding existing abilities.

    Progress in these directions should carry forward to later work. Test improvements on held-out tasks, check that existing abilities are retained, and identify what changed in the model, harness, or skills.

    Applications

    Imagine giving a robot an outcome, then leaving it to organize the work: breakfast served, shelves replenished, a facility inspected. Embodied S2 could make validated robot skills usable across a growing portfolio of jobs, allowing a deployment to become more valuable as it takes on more recurring work.

    Homes

    Daily household service. Prepare meals, reset rooms, and replenish essentials around a household’s routines and preferences. One robot could earn its place through several useful jobs every day.

    Manufacturing & Logistics

    Adaptable material flow. Coordinate picking, kitting, replenishment, and delivery; handle a missing part or an order change without restarting the whole workflow. Reusable skills could make the next production change cheaper to support.

    Inspection

    Inspection that leads to action. Cover a site, investigate unusual readings, and return with checked findings and evidence. Reliable rounds could extend coverage while reducing the time operators spend chasing uncertain alarms.

    Healthcare & Laboratories

    More time for care and discovery. Coordinate supply delivery, sample transport, and workstation preparation with explicit safety checks and traceable handovers. These support services could return staff time to patients and experiments.

    Agriculture & Mobility

    More jobs across a larger site. Move supplies, inspect crops or equipment, and adapt routes as conditions change. Sharing navigation and handling skills across assignments could keep a robot productive through more of the day.

    The commercial opportunity grows with the work a deployment can reliably absorb. More completed jobs per robot, less setup for the next workflow, and fewer human interventions are the measures that turn broader capability into a stronger business case.

    Conclusion

    Four Takeaways

    1. Foundation models make S2 a promising starting point for robot generalization.

      Their broad knowledge and reasoning can help robots understand unfamiliar goals and situations, organize available capabilities, and adapt through feedback, alongside advances in action-policy training.

    2. Physical autonomy requires the model and embodied harness to work together.

      They must support effective, verifiable action under constraints of time, embodiment, uncertainty, and safety. These constraints must shape reasoning, execution, and adaptation.

    3. Evaluation must distinguish model capabilities from system performance.

      Assess both what the model can do and how its capabilities contribute to reliable outcomes in the complete closed-loop system.

    4. The long-term goal is sustained autonomy with broader responsibility.

      Progress extends from individual primitives and tasks to coordinated workflows and standing roles, measured by the breadth and duration of work people can reliably entrust to robots.

    Three Priorities

    1. Develop reliable evaluation frameworks for broader responsibilities and sustained operation.

      Complement simulation with real-robot testing and cover higher levels of responsibility and broader spatial scopes. Assess sustained performance under changing conditions, including recovery from failure.

    2. Develop embodied-native reasoning that integrates perception, reasoning, and action.

      Improve decisions within the time available for action, rather than relying on longer deliberation alone. Work toward reasoning that integrates perception, reasoning, and action in response to the dynamics and urgency of physical tasks.

    3. Standardize reporting for reproducible comparisons and clear attribution of results.

      Make the model, harness, available capabilities, evaluation conditions, inference budgets, response times, and human assistance explicit. Enable reproducible comparisons and distinguish model capabilities from the support provided by the surrounding system.
